# 3. PROPOSED WORK

---

## 3.1 Problem Statement

Automated credit-scoring models present a tension between predictive
performance and interpretability. A high-performing model such as a
gradient-boosted ensemble or a regularised logistic regression trained on
hundreds of thousands of borrowers may produce a decision — approve or
decline a loan application — without any human-readable justification.
This opaqueness poses three distinct problems.

First, a rejected applicant cannot understand why they were declined and
therefore cannot take corrective action. Second, a loan officer reviewing
the model's output cannot audit whether the decision reflects genuine
financial risk or an artefact of training data bias. Third, regulators
increasingly require that such decisions be explainable in a form the
affected party can comprehend and challenge.

XAI tools such as SHAP and DiCE address different parts of this problem:
SHAP explains which features drove a decision, while DiCE identifies
what the applicant could change to receive a different outcome. Neither
tool, however, produces a natural-language explanation suitable for direct
communication to a borrower or a non-technical reviewer. LLMs can bridge
this gap — but the choice of which XAI data to provide to the LLM (SHAP
only, counterfactuals only, or both) has not been systematically evaluated
in the credit-risk domain.

## 3.2 Literature Review

The following review covers twelve sources relevant to the intersection of
explainable AI, credit risk, and natural-language explanation generation.
Each summary is followed by an explicit statement of the gap it leaves
open.

**[1]** A factorial study published in January 2026 investigates the
quality of natural-language explanations generated by LLMs when different
XAI techniques are provided as input, measuring how explanation quality
varies with the choice of input representation [1]. While this work
demonstrates that input type matters for LLM explanation quality, it does
not focus on the credit-risk domain or apply domain-specific feasibility
constraints to counterfactual inputs.

**[2]** An end-to-end XAI framework proposed for energy systems
integrates multiple explainability methods — including feature attribution
and counterfactual generation — within a single deployment pipeline [2].
However, the study does not evaluate the natural-language communication
of these explanations to end-users, nor does it compare single-method
versus combined-method explanation strategies.

**[3]** Work on generative explainability for next-generation networks
uses LLMs to translate structured model outputs into human-readable
explanations for network anomaly detection [3]. The domain is distinct
from credit risk, and the study does not compare SHAP-based and
counterfactual-based prompting strategies against each other.

**[4]** ContextualSHAP extends standard SHAP by incorporating contextual
information from the application domain into the attribution computation,
aiming to improve the relevance of feature importance rankings [4].
Although this advances SHAP-based explanation quality, it does not
address actionable recourse or LLM-mediated communication of its outputs.

**[5]** A systematic review of explainability approaches for
classification models published in MDPI in 2026 surveys and categorises
XAI methods across multiple domains, comparing their fidelity,
complexity, and applicability [5]. The review confirms that no consensus
exists on which explanation type most effectively serves non-technical
audiences in financial applications.

**[6]** A domain-specific review of explainable AI in anaesthesiology
provides a methodologically instructive template for how XAI can be
adapted to high-stakes decision support, emphasising the importance of
understandability for clinicians who are not data scientists [6]. The
study's insights regarding audience-appropriate explanation design are
relevant but do not translate directly to the credit domain or to
counterfactual recourse generation.

**[7]** An application of SHAP to student performance prediction,
published by Springer in 2026, demonstrates the technique's utility for
educational outcome modelling and provides worked examples of SHAP
feature importance visualisations [7]. The study covers neither
counterfactual explanations nor natural-language generation and does not
address actionability.

**[8]** A systematic literature review of 43 studies published between
2020 and 2025 examines AI-based credit scoring with attention to
explainability methods and regulatory adoption barriers [8]. The review
identifies that most existing work evaluates explainability tools in
isolation and that few studies assess whether explanations actually enable
borrowers to improve their financial profiles, pointing directly toward
the actionability gap this project addresses.

**[9]** A systematic review covering the period 2018 to 2026 on
AI-driven credit risk assessment identifies interpretability and
standardisation gaps as primary obstacles to widespread adoption of ML
in lending [9]. The review notes that existing benchmarks for explanation
quality in credit risk lack consensus metrics, and that the lack of
domain-specific feasibility constraints is a recurring weakness in
counterfactual approaches.

**[10]** A credit risk assessment paper that applies SHAP-based feature
explanation to lending decisions demonstrates empirically which features
most strongly predict default in a real lending portfolio [10]. The
work does not generate natural-language explanations or evaluate
actionability.

**[11]** A Springer paper on loan default prediction with XAI references
the Reserve Bank of India's responsible-AI framework as the regulatory
motivation for explainability in the Indian financial sector [11]. The
study treats explainability primarily as a compliance requirement rather
than a communication design problem, and does not compare different XAI
input types for LLM-mediated explanation generation.

**[12]** Bansal and Sharma propose a fusion of SHAP and LIME attributions
combined with actionability-constrained counterfactual explanations for
credit and healthcare risk models [12]. This is the closest related work
to the current project. However, it does not use an LLM for the natural-
language communication step and does not perform a controlled three-arm
comparison of explanation strategies. The current project extends this
line of work by adding LLM-mediated generation and a systematic
evaluation framework.

### Summary of the Gap

Across all twelve sources, a consistent pattern emerges: existing work
evaluates either feature attribution or counterfactual recourse, seldom
both together, and almost none applies a domain-constrained feasibility
filter before LLM prompting. No study found in this review performs a
controlled three-arm comparison (SHAP-only, CF-only, combined) of
LLM-generated credit-risk explanations using independent automated
metrics and a blinded human survey instrument.

## 3.3 Proposed Approach

### Three-Arm Experimental Design

The central methodological contribution of this project is a controlled
three-arm design in which the same five case-study instances receive
three independently generated natural-language explanations, each based
on a different XAI input:

- **Arm 1 — SHAP-only:** The LLM is given the top-five SHAP features,
  their values, and their directional contributions. The prompt explicitly
  instructs the model to explain *why* the decision was made and to
  refrain from providing actionable advice.

- **Arm 2 — Counterfactual-only:** The LLM is given the feasible
  counterfactual changes (after the directional feasibility filter). The
  prompt instructs the model to explain *what the applicant could do
  differently* without explaining why the original decision was made.

- **Arm 3 — Combined:** The LLM receives both SHAP and counterfactual
  data and is instructed to address both the why and the what-to-do
  within a single coherent explanation.

The same generation model (`openai/gpt-oss-120b`) and temperature (0.2)
are used for all three arms to isolate the effect of XAI input type.

### Feasibility Filtering

Before any LLM call, counterfactual suggestions from DiCE are passed
through a rule-based directional feasibility filter. This filter encodes
domain knowledge about which feature changes are realistically achievable:
late-payment counts and credit utilisation can only decrease; income can
only increase; age and number of dependents are immutable. Only
counterfactuals that satisfy all directional constraints are passed to the
LLM. Raw counterfactuals that require unrealistic changes — such as
increasing the number of late payments — are discarded.

### Evaluation Framework

The evaluation framework applies four independent measurement lenses to
the 15 generated explanations (5 instances × 3 arms):

1. **Rule-based faithfulness:** An automated check measures what
   proportion of the top-N ground-truth SHAP features are mentioned in
   the generated text (top-feature recall), using a feature-alias
   dictionary to handle paraphrase. A contamination check confirms that
   SHAP-only explanations do not contain actionable advice, and CF-only
   explanations do not contain feature-attribution reasoning.

2. **LLM-as-judge:** A separate model (`qwen/qwen3.8-27b`, deliberately
   different from the generation model) scores each explanation on three
   dimensions — faithfulness, understandability, and actionability —
   on a 1–10 scale, providing structured JSON output that is parsed and
   aggregated.

3. **Readability:** Flesch-Kincaid grade level and Flesch Reading Ease
   are computed for every explanation using the `textstat` library,
   providing an objective, model-independent readability measure.

4. **Human survey (pending):** A blinded HTML survey instrument assigns
   the three arms to random labels (A, B, C) per instance using a fixed
   random seed, ensuring participants cannot infer which arm produced
   which explanation. Participants rate each explanation on three
   Likert-scale items (understanding, trust, knows next step) and select
   a preference. The label-to-arm mapping is stored separately in
   `survey_key.json` and is applied only after responses are collected.
